# Segmentation rubric

Use this rubric after reading the entire chapter and before proposing IDs.

## Scene boundaries

Start a new scene when one or more strong signals change materially:

- physical production location;
- time period or meaningful time jump;
- immediate player objective;
- dramatic question or emotional purpose;
- controllable state, such as exploration to performance or transit;
- presentation mode or camera language;
- required reusable environment kit;
- transition that likely needs loading, streaming, or a new level instance.

Treat these as supporting rather than sufficient signals:

- a new conversation topic;
- a character entrance or exit;
- a new prose section;
- a pacing pause;
- a new interaction in the same space.

Do not split solely on paragraph, page, or dialogue breaks. Do not merge distinct
production locations merely because the prose transition is short.

For every boundary, name the signal that justifies it. If the only reason is
length, reconsider the boundary.

## Scene contents

Give each proposed scene:

- a stable uppercase snake-case ID;
- a concise title;
- a contiguous, ordered source range;
- a presentation mode supported by the editor;
- an immediate player goal;
- a dramatic purpose;
- an entry state and exit state;
- a boundary rationale that explains both why it begins and why it ends there.

Prefer source-order scenes. Treat reordering as a proposed story change requiring
author review.

## Beats

Create a beat when the player perceives or causes a meaningful state change:

- control begins or changes;
- an interaction reveals information;
- a character enters, exits, or changes behavior;
- source dialogue changes the dramatic question;
- an objective, choice, consequence, or transition becomes available;
- camera, audio, or staging must deliberately hold attention;
- a quiet observation is essential to tone or understanding.

Do not create a beat for every sentence. Combine prose that serves one dramatic
or playable function. Keep separate beats when order, trigger, optionality, or
consequence differs.

For every beat, state:

- the supporting paragraph IDs;
- what the player does, sees, or hears;
- why this needs its own beat;
- whether it is required or optional;
- whether it is source-supported or an adaptation proposal.

## Coverage dispositions

Classify every source paragraph exactly once:

- `direct`: represented substantially in a scene or source-locked dialogue;
- `condensed`: represented through shorter staging, summary, ambience, or a
  combined beat;
- `omitted`: intentionally absent from the playable first pass.

Do not use `omitted` merely because a paragraph lacks dialogue or action.
Preserve material that establishes mood, world rules, motivation, continuity, or
later payoff unless there is a concrete reason to condense it.

Give each condensed or omitted range a short source anchor and a specific
rationale. Avoid vague reasons such as “for pacing” without explaining the
actual pacing problem.

## Adaptation restraint

Separate these categories in the report:

- source-supported fact;
- staging inference required to present the source;
- optional game-design proposal;
- deliberate change from source;
- unresolved author decision.

Never disguise a proposal as canon. Never invent a proper noun, motivation,
inventory reward, branching consequence, or final line of dialogue to close a
gap.
